## Overview

Battery cells in an electric car heat up when they charge and discharge. Above about 45 °C they age faster and become less safe, so packs sit on a cold plate: a metal plate with coolant flowing through channels underneath.

Channel shape is a trade-off. Narrow, winding channels cool well but need a strong pump; wide, open ones are easy to pump but can leave hot spots. I asked which layout keeps the cells coolest and most even per watt of pumping power, and how fast a real cell could charge on it.

<div class="note">

All temperatures and pressures here are **simulated predictions**. The bench rig is designed and costed but **not yet built**, so nothing has been measured.

</div>

### The test module

I sized the module so it can be built and tested on a bench:

<div class="table-wrap">

| Part | Specification |
|---|---|
| Dummy cells | 4 × 6061-T6 aluminium blocks, 30 × 80 × 60 mm, 2 mm apart, each with a 12 V cartridge heater |
| Heat load | 10, 20 and 30 W per cell (40–120 W total) |
| Spreader | 6061-T6 sheet, 3.175 mm (1/8 in), 190 × 130 mm |

### Hand calculation first

Before any CFD, I built a lumped network for each cell (block → thermal paste → aluminium sheet → coolant), with the coolant temperature marched along the real flow path. I used Shah & London for laminar ducts, Gnielinski for transitional and turbulent flow, Zukauskas for the pin bank and K-factors for the minor losses. This gave me an independent number to compare the CFD against.

<Figure src="ev-battery-cold-plate/thermal-resistance.png" alt="Stacked horizontal bars of thermal resistance per cell for each design, split into block, paste, sheet plus convection, and coolant rise." caption="Hand calculation: where the temperature rise comes from at 0.5 L/min and 10 W per cell. Convection from the sheet dominates, and the parallel Z design is the worst." />

### CFD set-up

I set up steady conjugate heat transfer in Ansys Fluent 2026 R1 (Student):

- **Mesh:** polyhedral meshes with prism layers on the fluid walls.
- **Heat input:** a volumetric heat source in each cartridge heater.
- **Interfaces:** a thin-wall contact resistance for each paste joint.
- **Outer walls:** convection to ambient.
- **Flow model:** laminar below Re 2,300, k-ω SST above. At the serpentine's 0.5 L/min point (Re ≈ 2,070) I bracketed both models: SST is 0.9 K cooler with 7% more pressure drop, so I kept laminar as the conservative choice.

**Design sweep.** 3 designs × 4 flow rates × 3 heat loads gives 36 operating points, which I covered with 14 solves. With constant properties and the inlet at ambient temperature, the temperature rise is linear in heat load, so I solved each design and flow once at 10 W per cell and scaled it. A direct 30 W solve matched the scaled result to within 10⁻¹¹ K.

## Results

At 0.5 L/min, 10 W per cell and a 22 °C inlet:

<div class="table-wrap">

| Result | Serpentine | Parallel Z | Pin-fin |
|---|---|---|---|
| Max cell temperature rise above inlet | 4.27 K | 6.88 K | **4.40 K** |
| Plate pressure drop | 4,041 Pa | 329 Pa | **152 Pa** |
| Cell-to-cell temperature spread | **0.60 K** | 2.12 K | 0.99 K |
| Worst case over the sweep (0.25 L/min, 30 W per cell) | 38.4 °C | **47.8 °C** (over the 45 °C limit) | 41.3 °C |
| Hottest printed-plastic temperature, any point | 34.4 °C | 43.6 °C | 37.0 °C |

</div>

- **Pin-fin is the best trade-off.** It gives almost the same cell temperature as the serpentine at about 1/27 of the plate pressure drop, and the lowest cell temperature per watt of pump power below about 2 W.
- **Serpentine is the most uniform** (cell-to-cell spread ≤ 1.85 K even at 30 W per cell), but the most expensive to pump.
- **Parallel Z runs hottest.** Its middle channels get less flow. It is the only design to pass 45 °C, at the lowest flow and highest load.
- **The cold plate does not set the charge-rate limit.** Across all designs and flows, the estimated sustainable charge rate for a real EVE LF105 LFP cell is 1.19–1.34 C. The limit comes from heat conduction inside the tall cell.

## How I checked the results

<div class="table-wrap">

| Check | Result |
|---|---|
| Mesh independence (serpentine; 276,606 / 613,923 / 884,522 cells) | T_max 26.26 °C ± 0.03 K; Δp 3,932 Pa ± 161 Pa (±4%), as the half-spread of the three meshes |
| Formal GCI | Not reliable here: refinement ratio 1.13 (< 1.3) and oscillatory convergence. The Fs = 3 GCI (±0.68 K) is kept only as an upper bound |
| Energy balance, baseline fine mesh | −0.04% (40 W in; 39.20 W to coolant, 0.81 W to ambient) |
| Sweep convergence | 13 of 14 solves met every criterion, with final energy balance −0.26% to +0.23% |
| Non-converged solve | Serpentine at 1.5 L/min hit the 1,500-iteration cap (energy imbalance −1.8%). It is outside the planned pump's range and is not a rig test point |
| Heat-load scaling | Direct 30 W solve vs scaled 10 W solve: differences below 10⁻¹¹ K |
| Hand calc vs CFD (baseline) | T_max 26.50 vs 26.26 °C; Δp 2,617 vs 3,932 Pa |

</div>

<Chart id="cp-mesh" title="Mesh study, serpentine baseline" caption="Simulated T_max on three meshes; the half-spread is ±0.03 K." />

I also checked the planned rig against the CFD before building anything. Its predicted resolution is ±0.14 K on cell rise and ±3.6–6.3% on the energy balance (k = 2). That means pin-fin vs serpentine is only marginally resolvable at 0.5 L/min, so I made 1.0 L/min the deciding test.

## Challenges & what I learned

- **The default stopping rule stopped too early.** Fluent's default residual criterion ended solves before the temperatures had settled. I replaced it with a windowed test on the quantities I care about: T_max range below 0.01 K, outlet-temperature rise within 5%, Δp within 0.5% and energy imbalance below 1%.
- **Reversed flow biased the outlet temperature.** 4–18% of the outlet face had reversed flow, which pulled the face-averaged outlet temperature low. I switched to the mixed-cup value from energy conservation.
- **The cell limit forced a coarser pin-fin mesh.** The 338 pins needed 858,399 cells, close to the Student licence limit, so the pin-fin mesh is coarser than the serpentine study. By judgement I treat its uncertainty as about ±0.3 K and ±15% Δp.
- **Plastic pins are flow features, not fins.** Printed PETG has k ≈ 0.2 W/m·K, so the pins mainly stir the flow. A machined aluminium version is a possible next step.

## Limitations & next steps

- **Not yet validated.** The rig is designed and costed ($92 of parts to buy) and its ESP32 firmware compiles, but nothing has been built or measured.
- **Assumed inputs:** the paste-joint contact resistance is the largest unknown (worth −0.06 to +0.73 K at 10 W per cell); the real cell's DC resistance and in-plane conductivity; a placeholder pump curve.
- **Constant water properties.** One re-solve with temperature-dependent viscosity moved the rig sensor points by ≤ 0.04 K.

**The bench rig (designed, not built):** 8 NTC thermistors on two 16-bit ADCs, measured heater power (INA219), a flow sensor and a water manometer. I designed the ESP32 firmware (FreeRTOS) with latched safety trips, plus a data logger and an uncertainty budget.

**Planned:** print the three channel bodies, machine the blocks, build and leak-test the rig, measure the pump curve, run 3 designs × 0.5 and 1.0 L/min × 10 and 20 W per cell, and compare with the CFD per ASME V&V 20.
