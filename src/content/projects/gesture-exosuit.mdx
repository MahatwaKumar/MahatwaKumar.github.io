## Problem statement

Spinal cord injury, stroke and brachial plexus injury can leave a person unable to bend the elbow. Most powered braces take their commands from the hand, or from muscle signals (EMG) in the affected arm, and these users often have neither. Many of them keep good control of the jaw and neck.

A brace that listens to the teeth and head could give back simple, useful movements, such as bringing a cup towards the mouth. It also has to cost little and be easy to repair, so it can be built outside a lab.

The question for this phase: can a small cable drive lift the forearm of a large adult holding 0.5 kg with a sensible margin, and what happens to the arm if that drive fails?

<div class="note">

This page covers the **design phase** (6 of 13 milestones). No prototype has been built, so every performance number is a **design prediction** or a value taken from the CAD model, not a measurement on hardware.

</div>

- **For the control concept** I put a Seeed XIAO nRF52840 Sense board (with a motion sensor) and a piezo element in an ear pod. Three quick teeth taps within a short timing window arm the brace. After that, tilting the head forward lifts the forearm, tilting it back lowers it, and holding the head level (within ±5°) holds the arm still. The brace disarms itself after 8 s without a command. The arming step is meant to stop chewing, talking and ordinary head movement from moving the arm.
- **For the electronics** the pod sends commands over Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE) to an ESP32-S3 controller, which drives a BTS7960 H-bridge and reads a potentiometer at the elbow. A separate ATtiny85 watchdog opens a relay 0.25 s after the controller's heartbeat stops, so a controller fault cannot keep the motor running.
- **For the drive train** I chose a 12 V self-locking linear actuator in a hip pack. It pulls a Dyneema line through a 4:1 pulley block, then through a Bowden housing up the back and over the shoulder to a 50 mm pulley at the elbow. The 100 mm of cable stroke on the 50 mm pulley gives the 0 to 115° elbow range. Because the actuator self-locks, the arm holds its position if power is lost.
- **For the dynamics model** I treated the forearm and hand as one rigid segment using standard anthropometric ratios: mass 2.2% of body mass, length 14.6% of height, centre of mass at 68.2% of the length and radius of gyration 82.7%. I checked two users, a median adult (75 kg, 1.75 m) and a large adult (100 kg, 1.85 m), and added the brace's moving parts (0.25 kg at 0.12 m), 0.5 kg held in the hand and 2 rad/s² of elbow acceleration. From that I computed:
  - peak torque and the margin to the 8 N·m drive rating;
  - cable tension at the elbow and at the pack;
  - torque against elbow angle from 0 to 115°;
  - the work for a full lift, the free fall from 90° if the drive fails (stepped through time at 0.2 ms), and cuff contact pressure;
  - actuator force, rod travel, lift time and daily energy against the battery.
- **In SolidWorks** I modeled revision B: 42 parts in one assembly, posed at 90° of elbow flexion. It has contoured open C-shell cuffs with foam liners and magnetic-buckle straps, printed hinge bars on both sides of the elbow, a wrist splint, the hip pack with its electronics, and the ear pod on a neckband. I ran an interference check on the full assembly, took the mass properties of every part, rendered 14 views, made an assembly drawing with a bill of materials, and exported the 12 parts meant for 3D printing in PETG as STL.
- **To show the control behaviour** I animated the planned control logic on the real CAD model: a head and jaw seen from the side, and a live panel with elbow angle, torque, cable tension and sensor signals.

### How I checked it

- **Free-fall time-step convergence.** I reran the 90° to 0° fall with steps of 0.8, 0.4, 0.2, 0.1 and 0.05 ms and compared the final angular speed with the exact value from conservation of energy, ω = √(2·T<sub>g</sub>/I). The largest error was 0.00021% (median adult, 0.8 ms step), shrinking about 4× each time the step is halved. Fall times agree within 0.4 ms across all steps, so the 0.2 ms step I used is converged.
- **Kinematic consistency.** The 100 mm cable stroke on a 50 mm pulley gives 115° of elbow range, matching the range used for the torque and work calculations.
- **Sensitivity to user size.** I ran every load case for both users. The large adult sets the design margin: 1.26× on torque, and 912 N on the rod against a 1,500 N actuator.
- **Interference check in SolidWorks.** The first check of the full assembly found 26 overlaps. After I fixed them all, the final check found none.
- **CAD mass against the model.** I compared the mass properties of the moving brace parts with the value assumed in the sizing model (see above).
- **Not done yet:** no finite-element analysis, no physical tests, and no measured friction or cable stretch.

## Conclusion

On paper, yes: the cable drive can lift a large adult's forearm with 0.5 kg in the hand at a predicted 1.26× margin to its 8 N·m rating, or about 1.25× with the brace mass taken from CAD. The actuator works at 912 N of its 1,500 N. A full lift takes a predicted 3.6 s, and a day of use fits in the battery with room to spare. The drive-failure case is the more serious result: an unbraked forearm would drop in about 0.3 s, so the self-locking actuator, hard end stops and an independent watchdog are design requirements, not extras. Revision B now fits together in CAD with no interferences.

**Limitations:**

- **Nothing is measured on hardware yet.** All torques, forces, speeds and energies come from a simplified model; geometry and masses come from CAD.
- **Assumed values:** Bowden efficiency (0.78), pulley block efficiency (0.9), actuator power draw (24 W lifting, 8 W lowering) and the daily use pattern.
- **Simplified body model:** one rigid segment from population-average ratios, with no joint stiffness or spasticity. The cuff pressure assumes even contact.
- **The free-fall case** ignores the actuator and friction; it is a worst-case bound.
- **Tap detection is not tested.** The thresholds in the simulation are placeholders, and there is no measured false-trigger rate.
- **No full-system weight or priced bill of materials yet.** Parts without a material in CAD (electronics, actuator, foam, webbing) carry a placeholder density, and the printed parts haven't been sliced or test-printed.

**Next steps (planned):** test-print the cuffs, hinge bars and pulley and check the fit; static FEA of the printed pulley, cuffs and brackets at 160 to 205 N cable load; write the firmware (BLE, the arming logic, the position loop and the watchdog); record tapping, chewing and talking to measure false triggers; bench-test the drive for tension, efficiency and actuator stall current; build a priced bill of materials and weigh the full system.

## Issues I ran into

- **The failure case was worse than the normal case.** The model showed that if the drive fails, the forearm falls from 90° in about 0.3 s, reaching 2.3 m/s at the wrist. I made a self-locking actuator and hard end stops design requirements, and gave the watchdog its own relay so it cuts power independently of the controller.
- **An "average" user hid the real margin.** The median user has a comfortable 1.60× margin, but the large adult drops it to 1.26×. I ran every load case for both users and sized the drive to the large adult.
- **Two lift-speed figures disagreed.** My sizing model gave an elbow speed of 0.50 rad/s (29 °/s) and a 4.0 s full lift in one place, but working through the actual drive (7 mm/s rod speed, ×4 through the pulley block, on the 50 mm pulley) gave 32 °/s and 3.6 s. I report the drive-based figures, since they follow from the actuator and pulley I chose.
- **The first concept needed a revision.** Revision A used a steel bike-brake cable, a back plate and a mushroom emergency-stop button. Revision B moves to PETG printed parts, Dyneema line in a Bowden housing, a hip pack and an inline power switch.
- **26 interferences in the first check.** Axle holes, the clevis, wiring leads, housing runs, a sensor bracket and standoffs overlapped. I fixed each one until the check came back clean.
- **SolidWorks crashed and hung.** It crashed once during a feature cut and hung several times on large rebuilds of the assembly. I split the work into separate stages (assembly, rendering, checks) so each could be redone on its own.
- **Fillets and a misplaced part.** Some fillets would not build at first; selecting the edges directly fixed them. One forearm bar first came out 52 mm out of place because the wrong body was picked up after a feature; I corrected it and checked the placement of each new body.
- **False triggers are the real control risk.** Chewing and talking also move the jaw, so I added an arming pattern (three taps in a window) and a timeout. The detector's false-trigger rate still has to be measured on real data.
