## Overview

Robot arms are expensive mainly because of their motors and the engineering time needed to program every movement. A student or a small lab can't easily get an arm to experiment with.

I designed Iris Claw to be cheap enough to print on makerspace printers, light enough to run on hobby servos, and described precisely enough (as a CAD model and a kinematic model) to be used later in a physics simulator, where control policies can be trained before they run on the real hardware.

<div class="note">

The design phase is complete. All numbers are **predicted** from the CAD model and a static calculation; nothing has been printed or measured yet.

</div>

### Design choices

- **Arm:** 6 revolute joints in an articulated layout (J1 base yaw, J2 shoulder pitch, J3 elbow pitch, J4 forearm roll, J5 wrist pitch, J6 hand roll), giving full position and orientation of the hand.
- **Joints:** each pitch servo sits in a window in one link's cheek plate and drives the next link through a recessed horn. An M4 idler pin carries the opposite side, so bending loads go into the pin, not the servo output shaft.
- **Hand:** 4 fingers × 3 segments. Each finger is driven by one MG90S micro servo pulling an antagonistic tendon pair (flex and extend). Fingers 2 and 4 sit on a servo-driven swivel ring (up to 55°), so the claw can switch between wrap, pinch and gesture grips. A camera mount sits in the centre of the palm.
- **Actuators:** DS3218 (J1, J3), DS3235 (J2), MG996R (J4, J5) and MG90S (J6, 4 finger drives, swivel): 11 servos on a PCA9685 PWM driver and a 6 V supply.
- **Materials:** PLA structure, PETG accent rings, TPU fingertip pads, steel pins and a lazy-susan bearing at the base.

Worst case means the arm stretched horizontally with a 100 g object at the claw tips. Safety factor = servo rated torque ÷ required static torque.

<Chart id="ic-torque" title="Required torque vs servo rating" caption="Predicted, static, arm horizontal with a 100 g payload. The shoulder needs 81% of its servo's rated torque." />

**The shoulder is the critical joint.** At full horizontal reach it needs 81% of its servo's rated torque. I've proposed three changes to raise that margin: a spring counterbalance at the shoulder, lighter infill in the links, and an upgrade of the elbow servo to a DS3235, which shares the same body size. These fixes are designed on paper only; they are not yet in the CAD model or the analysis.

## How I checked the results

- **Assembly placement check.** I compared the bounding box of every component as placed in SolidWorks with the box predicted by my kinematic model. The worst difference on the idle pose was **0.00 mm** over 47 components, which confirms the transform convention used for every pose and for the torque analysis.
- **Single-body check.** I confirmed every part is one solid body, which catches features that leave loose fragments. The only exception is the upper-arm side panels, which are two separate panels by design.
- **Materials and colours.** After saving, I read every part's appearance values back to confirm they were stored.
- **Hand check of the masses.** The servo total (375.4 g) matches the catalogue masses, and the printed mass is the sum of the individual parts.
- **Not done:** no finite-element analysis, mesh study or physical test. The torques are static (no acceleration, friction or tendon loads).

## Challenges & what I learned

- **The shoulder margin is too thin.** A safety factor of 1.24 is low for a hobby servo whose real torque is often below its datasheet value. Finding this on paper, before printing, is exactly why I did the analysis first.
- **No PLA in the material library.** SolidWorks had no PLA, so the printed parts carry ABS as their CAD material, and I used the PLA density (1.24 g/cm³) and a 50% fill factor in the mass analysis instead.
- **Posed, not mated.** I place each component from the kinematic model and fix it, rather than using mates, so the three poses match the kinematics exactly. The trade-off is that the joints can't be dragged in SolidWorks.
- **Servo bodies are from datasheets.** I modeled them from typical datasheet dimensions, and they need checking against the real servos before printing.

## Limitations & next steps

- **No hardware yet.** Nothing has been printed or assembled.
- **Static analysis only.** Dynamic loads from acceleration, gearbox backlash, tendon friction and servo heating are not modelled.
- **Assumed mass factor:** real slicer weights may differ from the 50% fill assumption.

**Planned (not started):** printing and assembly; wiring and servo calibration; export to URDF and a digital twin in NVIDIA Isaac Sim; webcam hand-tracking teleoperation; reinforcement and imitation learning of tasks.
