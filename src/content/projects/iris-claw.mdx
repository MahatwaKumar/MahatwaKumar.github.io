## Problem statement

Robot arms are expensive, mainly because of their motors and the engineering time it takes to program every movement. That puts them out of reach for a student or a small lab that wants one to experiment with.

I set out to design an arm that is:

- **cheap**, with parts printed on makerspace printers and driven by hobby servos;
- **light enough** for those servos to hold it at full reach while carrying a 100 g object;
- **defined precisely enough**, as a CAD model and a kinematic model, to drop into a physics simulator later, so control can be trained before it runs on the real arm.

The question for this phase: can every joint hold its worst-case static load with the servos I picked, and if not, which joint fails first?

The CAD and the simulations are complete and the first parts are printing. Every number on this page is **predicted** from the CAD model and a static calculation, or **simulated** in Isaac Sim. The only measurement so far is a printed fit-test coupon.

I started from one shared table of dimensions (servo bodies, link lengths, finger segments), so a change to one number carries through every part that uses it.

- **In SolidWorks** I modeled all 29 parts as parametric parts and gave each one its material and colour. I built three posed assemblies (idle, peace sign, power grip) of 47 components each, made an A3 general-arrangement drawing with a ballooned bill of materials, and took the mass properties of every part for the load analysis.
- **For the kinematics** I described the arm as a chain of 4×4 homogeneous transforms, from the base yaw joint out to each finger segment. Every component in each SolidWorks assembly sits exactly where this model puts it for that pose's joint angles.
- **For the loads** I took the arm's worst static case: stretched out horizontally with a 100 g object at the claw tips. I summed the moment of every link, servo and the payload about each pitch joint, and compared the result with that servo's rated torque.
- **In NVIDIA Isaac Sim 5.1** I imported the arm as a URDF robot description, with each link's mass, inertia and centre of mass taken from the SolidWorks parts. Each joint drive is set up to behave like a hobby servo: it reaches stall torque at 3° of position error, and it has damping tuned from step tests, a rated speed limit and a deadband. Stall torques are datasheet values at 5 V. The finger middle and tip joints follow the knuckle, standing in for the tendon. I ran the physics at 240 Hz under gravity:
  - **Reference poses** with the original servo set: 5 poses, each held for 3 s.
  - **Collision sweep:** I moved the parts along a full motion tour and checked every pair of parts for overlap.
  - **Cheaper-servo study:** 2,135 tests for a budget servo set (DS3218, MG996R and MG90S; stall torques 1.77, 0.95 and 0.18 N·m). These were servo step tests, a 1,567-pose static sweep, a search for the lowest-strain posture for 48 hand targets, 12 motion profiles on 5 moves, lighter-part scaling and robustness variants.
- **In PrusaSlicer** I prepared the parts for printing and printed a fit-test coupon on the target printer to set the hole sizes for pins and filament.

### Design choices

- **Arm:** 6 revolute joints in an articulated layout (J1 base yaw, J2 shoulder pitch, J3 elbow pitch, J4 forearm roll, J5 wrist pitch, J6 hand roll), giving full position and orientation of the hand.
- **Joints:** each pitch servo sits in a window in one link's cheek plate and drives the next link through a recessed horn. An M4 idler pin carries the opposite side, so bending loads go into the pin, not the servo output shaft.
- **Hand:** 4 fingers × 3 segments. Each finger is driven by one MG90S micro servo pulling an antagonistic tendon pair (flex and extend). Fingers 2 and 4 sit on a servo-driven swivel ring (up to 55°), so the claw can switch between wrap, pinch and gesture grips. A camera mount sits in the centre of the palm.
- **Actuators:** DS3218 (J1, J3), DS3235 (J2), MG996R (J4, J5) and MG90S (J6, 4 finger drives, swivel): 11 servos on a PCA9685 PWM driver and a 6 V supply.
- **Materials:** PLA structure, PETG accent rings, TPU fingertip pads, steel pins and a lazy-susan bearing at the base.

### Simulation in Isaac Sim

All values in this section are **simulated**, with no payload unless stated.

**Reference poses (original servo set).** All 5 poses held within 0.81° of their targets. The shoulder needed 1.82 N·m at full horizontal reach, against 3.43 N·m for the DS3235. That run has no payload, so it isn't directly comparable with the 2.78 N·m from the hand calculation above, which includes 100 g at the claw.

Fully stretched out, the shoulder needs 103% of its stall torque and the elbow 83%, so the cheaper set can't hold that pose. The wrist never goes above 15% of stall. The safe band is where the forearm points roughly upwards (shoulder and elbow angles summing to about zero). From this map I set software limits ("gentle mode") that keep every joint at or below 40% of stall for each shoulder angle.

**Motion profiles.** On a base sweep, a trapezoid profile of 200 °/s and 800 °/s² finished the move in 0.85 s at about 63% peak load and 0.37° overshoot. A raw step command from home to the idle pose instead spiked above 5× stall with 10° of overshoot, so every move is commanded through a profile.

**Posture, mass and robustness.**

- Choosing the lowest-strain hand angle and elbow bend for a target cut the worst joint load by 5 percentage points on average, and by 23 at best, over 48 hand targets.
- Cutting the printed-part mass by 15% lowers the joint torques by about 10%, and a 30% cut by about 19%.
- The safe zone survives a 20% weaker servo or 20% heavier parts: 10 of its 11 test poses still held within 2°. The brief zone doesn't: with both changes, only 1 of 20 held.
- In the collision sweep the claws touch at a knuckle angle of −5.8° (4.5 mm of overlap at −8°), so I limited the knuckle range to −5° to +30°.

**Fit test (measured).** I printed a tolerance coupon on the target printer. The D-shaped press hole needed +0.05 mm and the filament press hole +0.15 mm (1.85 to 2.0 mm). Both changes are in the current design. I also reworked the printed parts (version 5) so that every piece prints in under 8.5 h.

### How I checked it

- **Assembly placement check.** I compared the bounding box of every component as placed in SolidWorks with the box predicted by my kinematic model. The worst difference on the idle pose was **0.00 mm** over 47 components, which confirms the placement used for every pose and for the torque analysis.
- **Single-body check.** I confirmed every part is one solid body, which catches features that leave loose fragments. The only exception is the upper-arm side panels, which are two separate panels by design.
- **Materials and colours.** After saving, I checked every part's appearance values to confirm they were stored.
- **Hand check of the masses.** The servo total (375.4 g) matches the catalogue masses, and the printed mass is the sum of the individual parts.
- **Simulation model against CAD.** Placing every part with the robot description's joint chain matched the SolidWorks placements within 0.005 mm, so the simulated arm has the same geometry as the CAD.
- **Static hand calculation against simulation.** Both put the shoulder as the most loaded joint, then the elbow, with the wrist far below.
- **Not done:** no finite-element analysis, mesh study or physical test of the arm. The hand-calculated torques are static (no acceleration, friction or tendon loads).

## Conclusion

The design works on paper: with the servos I chose, every joint can hold the stretched-out arm and a 100 g object. **The shoulder is the critical joint**, at a predicted safety factor of 1.24. That is too thin for a hobby servo, whose real torque is often below its datasheet value. The elbow (1.44) and wrist (2.77) have more margin.

The Isaac Sim tests agree and add the operating rules. With the original servos, the arm held all 5 reference poses within 0.81°. With the cheaper set, only 17% of poses are safe for continuous use, the stretched-out pose can't be held at all, and safe operation means keeping the forearm roughly upright, moving through smooth profiles rather than raw steps, and staying inside the gentle-mode limits. Lighter parts help almost one-for-one: 15% less printed mass gives about 10% less joint torque.

To raise the shoulder margin I've also proposed a spring counterbalance at the shoulder and an upgrade of the elbow servo to a DS3235, which shares the same body size. These are on paper only.

**Limitations:**

- **Almost no hardware data.** Only the fit-test coupon is measured. Grip force, finger speed, tendon friction, joint accuracy, heating and the gap between simulation and the real arm are unknown.
- **Datasheet servos.** Stall torques are datasheet values; real budget servos vary.
- **Not modelled:** gearbox backlash, servo heating and tendon friction. The fingers are idealised joints that follow the knuckle.
- **No payload in the sweep.** The safe-zone map is a static hold with an empty claw; any payload moves every zone towards higher load.
- **Self-collision was off in the physics runs.** Collisions were checked separately with the mesh sweep.
- **Older servo set in the tour video.** The pose tour uses the original servos; the cheaper-servo results and the version 5 printable parts came later.
- **Assumed mass factor.** Real slicer weights may differ from the 50% fill assumption.

**Next steps:** finish printing and assembly; wiring and servo calibration; measure joint accuracy and compare with the simulation; webcam hand-tracking teleoperation; reinforcement and imitation learning of tasks.

## Issues I ran into

- **The shoulder margin came out too thin.** The analysis showed the shoulder servo at 81% of its rating before any dynamic load. Finding this on paper, before printing, is exactly why I did the analysis first.
- **No PLA in the SolidWorks material library.** The printed parts carry ABS as their CAD material, so SolidWorks' own mass values don't match a PLA print. I used the PLA density (1.24 g/cm³) and a 50% fill factor in the mass analysis instead.
- **SolidWorks crashed when I reopened the posed assemblies.** Closing all three and reopening them crashed SolidWorks 2026, so I saved each pose's renders straight from the open assembly instead.
- **Posed, not mated.** To make the three poses match the kinematic model exactly, I fixed each component in place rather than using mates. The trade-off is that the joints can't be dragged in SolidWorks to try new poses.
- **Servo bodies are from datasheets.** I modeled them from typical datasheet dimensions. They need checking against the real servos before printing, or the cheek-plate windows may not fit.
- **Exported meshes came out shifted.** The STL files from SolidWorks landed in positive coordinate space instead of where the parts sit. I moved each mesh back by the difference between its centroid and the part's centre of mass from CAD.
- **The joints locked up in Isaac Sim.** The friction values in the robot description became joint friction that grows with load, so the loaded joints stuck. I set friction to zero and used damping for the losses instead.
- **The arm sagged under gravity.** The drives Isaac Sim created on import were far softer than a hobby servo. I switched to force drives with explicit stiffness and damping and a small armature. Before that, a wrong articulation root had left every joint frozen.
- **A false clash at the hand roll.** The collision sweep reported a clash because the servo horn was modeled as part of the servo body instead of turning with the palm. Once I attached it to the right part, the clash disappeared.
- **Two of my test rules were wrong.** My first rule for choosing damping picked the wrong value for the elbow servo, because that joint was at stall in the test pose and never settled; I changed it to ignore saturated joints. A "settled within 1°" rule also failed, because a loaded servo sits about 2° off target by design, so I judged moves by overshoot and peak load instead.
- **The laptop was below Isaac Sim's recommended spec.** It has an RTX 4060 with 8 GB. It ran, but the older graphics driver blocked hardware video encoding, so the videos were encoded on the CPU.
- **PrusaSlicer quietly ignored my printer profile** and produced generic print files. I caught it by checking the header of the output file, and fixed it by selecting the printer maker's full profile.
