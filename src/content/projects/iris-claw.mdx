## Problem statement

Robot arms are expensive, mainly because of their motors and the engineering time it takes to program every movement. That puts them out of reach for a student or a small lab that wants one to experiment with.

I set out to design an arm that is:

- **cheap**, with parts printed on makerspace printers and driven by hobby servos;
- **light enough** for those servos to hold it at full reach while carrying a 100 g object;
- **defined precisely enough**, as a CAD model and a kinematic model, to drop into a physics simulator later, so control can be trained before it runs on the real arm.

The question for this phase: can every joint hold its worst-case static load with the servos I picked, and if not, which joint fails first?

<div class="note">

The design phase is complete. All numbers are **predicted** from the CAD model and a static calculation; nothing has been printed or measured yet.

I started from one shared table of dimensions (servo bodies, link lengths, finger segments), so a change to one number carries through every part that uses it.

- **In SolidWorks** I modeled all 29 parts as parametric parts and gave each one its material and colour. I built three posed assemblies (idle, peace sign, power grip) of 47 components each, made an A3 general-arrangement drawing with a ballooned bill of materials, and took the mass properties of every part for the load analysis.
- **For the kinematics** I described the arm as a chain of 4×4 homogeneous transforms, from the base yaw joint out to each finger segment. Every component in each SolidWorks assembly sits exactly where this model puts it for that pose's joint angles.
- **For the loads** I took the arm's worst static case: stretched out horizontally with a 100 g object at the claw tips. I summed the moment of every link, servo and the payload about each pitch joint, and compared the result with that servo's rated torque.

### Design choices

- **Arm:** 6 revolute joints in an articulated layout (J1 base yaw, J2 shoulder pitch, J3 elbow pitch, J4 forearm roll, J5 wrist pitch, J6 hand roll), giving full position and orientation of the hand.
- **Joints:** each pitch servo sits in a window in one link's cheek plate and drives the next link through a recessed horn. An M4 idler pin carries the opposite side, so bending loads go into the pin, not the servo output shaft.
- **Hand:** 4 fingers × 3 segments. Each finger is driven by one MG90S micro servo pulling an antagonistic tendon pair (flex and extend). Fingers 2 and 4 sit on a servo-driven swivel ring (up to 55°), so the claw can switch between wrap, pinch and gesture grips. A camera mount sits in the centre of the palm.
- **Actuators:** DS3218 (J1, J3), DS3235 (J2), MG996R (J4, J5) and MG90S (J6, 4 finger drives, swivel): 11 servos on a PCA9685 PWM driver and a 6 V supply.
- **Materials:** PLA structure, PETG accent rings, TPU fingertip pads, steel pins and a lazy-susan bearing at the base.

### How I checked it

- **Assembly placement check.** I compared the bounding box of every component as placed in SolidWorks with the box predicted by my kinematic model. The worst difference on the idle pose was **0.00 mm** over 47 components, which confirms the placement used for every pose and for the torque analysis.
- **Single-body check.** I confirmed every part is one solid body, which catches features that leave loose fragments. The only exception is the upper-arm side panels, which are two separate panels by design.
- **Materials and colours.** After saving, I checked every part's appearance values to confirm they were stored.
- **Hand check of the masses.** The servo total (375.4 g) matches the catalogue masses, and the printed mass is the sum of the individual parts.
- **Not done:** no finite-element analysis, mesh study or physical test. The torques are static (no acceleration, friction or tendon loads).

## Conclusion

The design works on paper: with the servos I chose, every joint can hold the stretched-out arm and a 100 g object. **The shoulder is the critical joint**, at a predicted safety factor of 1.24. That is too thin for a hobby servo, whose real torque is often below its datasheet value. The elbow (1.44) and wrist (2.77) have more margin.

To raise the shoulder margin I've proposed three changes: a spring counterbalance at the shoulder, lighter infill in the links, and an upgrade of the elbow servo to a DS3235, which shares the same body size. These are on paper only; they are not yet in the CAD model or the analysis.

**Limitations:**

- **No hardware yet.** Nothing has been printed or assembled.
- **Static analysis only.** Dynamic loads from acceleration, gearbox backlash, tendon friction and servo heating are not modelled.
- **Assumed mass factor.** Real slicer weights may differ from the 50% fill assumption.

**Next steps (not started):** printing and assembly; wiring and servo calibration; export to URDF and a digital twin in NVIDIA Isaac Sim; webcam hand-tracking teleoperation; reinforcement and imitation learning of tasks.

## Issues I ran into

- **The shoulder margin came out too thin.** The analysis showed the shoulder servo at 81% of its rating before any dynamic load. Finding this on paper, before printing, is exactly why I did the analysis first.
- **No PLA in the SolidWorks material library.** The printed parts carry ABS as their CAD material, so SolidWorks' own mass values don't match a PLA print. I used the PLA density (1.24 g/cm³) and a 50% fill factor in the mass analysis instead.
- **SolidWorks crashed when I reopened the posed assemblies.** Closing all three and reopening them crashed SolidWorks 2026, so I saved each pose's renders straight from the open assembly instead.
- **Posed, not mated.** To make the three poses match the kinematic model exactly, I fixed each component in place rather than using mates. The trade-off is that the joints can't be dragged in SolidWorks to try new poses.
- **Servo bodies are from datasheets.** I modeled them from typical datasheet dimensions. They need checking against the real servos before printing, or the cheek-plate windows may not fit.
